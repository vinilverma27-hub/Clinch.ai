# Speaking script – B.Tech Project Final Presentation (7 minutes)

Kanishka Saraf (2023CE11023) · Alum coagulation of hostel drain wastewater

About 950 words. At a normal speaking pace (135–145 words a minute) this runs 6:40–7:00. Times in brackets are cumulative targets. Lines in *italics* are stage directions, not to be read.

**Slide 1 – Title  [0:00 – 0:30]**

Good morning. I am Kanishka Saraf, and this is my B.Tech project on alum coagulation for the wastewater from the Kailash Hostel drain, done under Prof. Arun Kumar with Swapnil Saha as my mentor.

At the mid-term I had shown the effect of pH and of alum dose separately. After that review the plan changed: the pH question was already answered by chemistry, so the second half of the project went into two things – fixing the alum dose, and dealing with the sludge that coagulation leaves behind.

**Slide 2 – Problem statement  [0:30 – 1:15]**

*Point to the map.* This is the Kailash Hostel area. Its wastewater runs in an open drain straight to the campus sewer with no treatment at all. It is mostly greywater – bathrooms, wash areas and the mess – so it carries suspended and colloidal solids, grease, and a lot of dissolved salt.

Alum coagulation is a simple option for a small unit at the outlet. But two things make it tricky. First, the dose: too little does nothing, too much actually makes the water worse. Second, everything you remove from the water, plus the aluminium hydroxide you create, ends up as a very wet sludge. Whether that sludge can be handled decides whether the scheme is practical.

So the question for the second half was: what dose, how much sludge, and what do we do with it?

**Slide 3 – Objectives  [1:15 – 1:45]**

Five objectives. The first two – characterising the water and screening pH and dose – were done before the mid-term. The last three are new: optimise the alum dose at pH 7 and pH 8, measure the sludge produced at that dose, and assess how it can be thickened, used or disposed of based on what we measure.

**Slide 4 – Methodology  [1:45 – 2:30]**

All tests were jar tests, 500 millilitre jars on a gang stirrer. Rapid mix at 120 rpm for two minutes to disperse the alum; slow mix at 30 rpm for thirty minutes to grow floc; then thirty minutes of settling. Supernatant was drawn three centimetres below the surface for turbidity and conductivity.

For the sludge, I siphoned off the supernatant, settled the sludge again in a graduated cylinder to read its volume, and dried it at 103 to 105 degrees for dry mass, moisture and solids content.

Three series were run: pH at a fixed dose, dose at native pH, and after the mid-term, 30 to 60 milligrams per litre at pH 7 and again at pH 8, with a no-alum control.

**Slide 5 – Literature  [2:30 – 3:10]**

This curve is why the pH–dose matrix was dropped. It is the solubility of aluminium hydroxide against pH. It is least soluble around pH 6 to 7, which is where the most sweep floc forms. Below pH 5.5 very little floc forms; above 8.5 the aluminate ion forms and floc redissolves. So the useful window is about 6.5 to 8, and that is settled chemistry.

On sludge, the literature says fresh alum floc is only 0.1 to 2 percent solids and dewaters badly, so thickening and drying beds come first. For reuse, dried sludge has been put into clay bricks at 5 to 15 percent, used as a soil amendment, and used as a phosphorus sorbent. In India it is not a hazardous waste and falls under the Solid Waste Management Rules, which favour reuse over landfill.

**Slide 6 – Field results  [3:10 – 3:40]**

Along the drain, dissolved oxygen is 1.24 milligrams per litre at the hostel outlet – essentially septic – and jumps to 4.8 and 4.5 just below the two step drops before falling again in the flat reaches. So the drain re-aerates itself at the drops, but what leaves the hostel is septic.

TDS was 2300 to 3200 and conductivity up to 5000 microsiemens. That salt load coagulation will not remove. The target is the suspended and colloidal fraction: about 39 NTU of turbidity and 39 to 78 milligrams per litre of TSS.

**Slide 7 – Screening  [3:40 – 4:10]**

These are the mid-term results. On the left, turbidity goes from 4.55 NTU at pH 5 down to 1.01 at pH 7.1 – about 97 percent removal – and back up to 2.9 at pH 8.1. That is the solubility curve showing up in the jar.

On the right, at native pH, turbidity falls from 3.4 at 10 milligrams per litre to 2.16 at 40, then rises again at 50 and 60. Classic under-dose, optimum, over-dose.

**Slide 8 – Dose at pH 7 and pH 8  [4:10 – 5:00]**

This is the main result. At pH 7, in orange, turbidity drops steadily to a clear minimum of 2.12 NTU at 50 milligrams per litre and then rises at 60 – so there is nothing to gain beyond 50. At pH 8, in blue, the curve is flat between 2.7 and 2.9 and is worse than pH 7 at every dose above 30. Part of the aluminium stays dissolved as aluminate at pH 8, so adding more alum gives less floc.

Conductivity on the right dips at exactly the same dose as turbidity in both series, which supports the choice. The no-alum control settled to 4.6 NTU, so plain settling removes the settleable solids and alum takes out the colloidal remainder.

The screening had given 40, this series gave 50 – the samples differed – so the recommendation is a working window of 40 to 50 milligrams per litre at pH 7 to 7.5. TSS went from 39–78 down to 2–4 milligrams per litre, over 90 percent removal.

**Slide 9 – Sludge quantity  [5:00 – 5:40]**

Now the sludge. At the working dose I got 2 to 10 millilitres of settled sludge per litre treated, 46 to 84 milligrams of dry solids, and a moisture content of 98.5 to 99.8 percent. So it is almost entirely water, and its volume is under one percent of the flow.

The mass balance on the right closes: the TSS removed from the water plus the aluminium hydroxide from the alum – about 10 milligrams per litre – gives 44 to 86 against 46 to 84 measured. That also tells us only 10 to 25 percent of the solids is hydroxide; the rest is organic matter from the wastewater. So this sludge is putrescible and must be stabilised, but its aluminium content is low.

Per 100 cubic metres treated, that is about 5 kilograms of alum and 5 to 8 kilograms of dry solids – a few buckets of cake once dried.

**Slide 10 – Sludge management  [5:40 – 6:20]**

The handling route: draw the sludge daily, never back into the drain; gravity-thicken for a day to 2 to 4 percent solids, returning the supernatant to the inlet; dry on a covered sand bed to over 20 percent; test the cake for aluminium, phosphorus, metals and coliforms; then use it.

Of the options, co-composting with campus green waste is the best fit – the organic fraction is an asset, aluminium is low and Delhi soils are alkaline. Blending 5 to 10 percent into clay bricks is a good alternative. Use as a phosphorus sorbent is secondary because the hydroxide fraction is small, and aluminium recovery is not worth it at 3.5 grams per cubic metre. The campus STP is the fallback, especially in the monsoon.

**Slide 11 – Conclusions  [6:20 – 6:45]**

To sum up. The outlet water is septic with a heavy salt load. Alum works best at pH 7, as the chemistry predicts. The optimum dose is 40 to 50 milligrams per litre; 60 is counter-productive and pH 8 is poorer throughout. Treated water is well inside discharge standards. The sludge is small in quantity, almost all water, and mostly organic rather than hydroxide. Thicken and dry it, then compost it on campus, with bricks as the alternative and the STP as fallback.

**Slide 12 – Future work  [6:45 – 7:00]**

Next steps: BOD and COD at the working dose, replicate runs to tighten the optimum, full sludge chemistry, a drying-bed and a brick trial, gauging the hostel flow to size a unit, and a comparison with polyaluminium chloride. The goal is a small pilot at the Kailash outlet with a compost outlet for the sludge.

Thank you. I am happy to take questions.

## Likely questions – short answers

- **Why did the optimum move from 40 to 50 mg/L?** Different grab samples; the sweep-floc dose depends on the solids present that day. Hence a 40–50 window and a periodic jar test rather than a fixed number.
- **Why is pH 8 flat?** Part of the aluminium is held in solution as aluminate, so extra alum adds proportionately less settleable hydroxide and the floc is lighter.
- **Control jar conductivity (941 µS/cm) is much lower than the dosed jars – why?** Alum alone cannot explain it (30 mg/L adds only tens of µS/cm). Most likely a sampling or meter issue; it is reported but not used in comparisons.
- **How was the Al(OH)₃ figure obtained?** Stoichiometry: 2 mol Al(OH)₃ per mol alum, 0.262 mg per mg for the 14-hydrate, 0.234 for the 18-hydrate; 40 mg/L gives 9.4–10.5 mg/L.
- **Why 500 mL jars?** Standard gang-stirrer beakers and the volume available from grab samples; the small sludge quantities are why values are ranges.
- **Why not PAC from the start?** Alum is cheaper and more available locally; PAC comparison is listed as future work.
- **BOD removal?** Not measured within the project period; listed first in future work.
